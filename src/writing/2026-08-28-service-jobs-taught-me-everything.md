As someone fast approaching the big 3-0, I am starting to feel nostalgic seeing the decade that many consider “the best of your life” come to an end.

Italian singer-songwriter Motta wrote a song a while ago (here on [Spotify](https://open.spotify.com/track/1R6VvUPobIdxPVCQNtPxYd) and [Apple Music](https://music.apple.com/be/song/la-fine-dei-ventanni/1523876426) if you’d rather listen to it later):

_La fine dei vent'anni / è un po' come essere in ritardo_

The end of your twenties is a bit like being late—he sings.

_Non devi sbagliare strada/ non farti del male/ e trovare parcheggio_

Just don't take the wrong road, don't hurt yourself, and find somewhere to park.

If this doesn't capture the anxiety of approaching 30, I don't know what does. You're both exhausted by the chaos your twenties threw at you and paralysed by the fact that you should have figured out more by now. No one's hyped for turning 30 like they are for 20. Twenty marks the start of an adventure, and 30 sort of marks the end of one.

You've been performing the person everyone expects you to be while trying to find yourself. You've spent years saying screw it, let's get out of here, I've had enough. But, as predicted, you've stayed, sometimes returned, because there's always someone to kiss, a dream to rescue, a wounded child to tend to.

There in the top ten burdens of my late twenties is the recognition that I've accumulated enough people and stories to fill a feature-length documentary. I've made too many quick decisions, lived through too many absurd stories, met too many people. The plane is about to take off but I'm anchored down by memories, and too many unresolved ones.

What from all this do I take with me into the next chapter?

One of the things that felt like taking the wrong road, the one Motta sings about, was jumping between one too many jobs through my twenties. Since 18 I've had, let's count, 13 jobs across two countries, four cities, and many offsite locations. I'm 29 now. Feel free to judge me. Some of those jobs I loved every minute of, others I showed up at work ready to disassociate for the following 8 hours. And trust me, I'm learning to understand where I fucked up, when my lack of patience took over, and why I love beginnings more than middles. But also where others let me down, what hurt me, and what type of jobs make me feel most fulfilled.

The jobs that taught me the most weren't the reasonably-paid joint head of something roles with too many meetings. They were the minimum wage, often part-time positions I took on before, during and after university. I worked in cafés, restaurants, gift shops, fast-food chains, receptions, food trucks and apothecaries. That's where I'd be face-to-face with customers, running around like crazy, working with small teams and side by side with the boss.

In the spirit of figuring out what has been valuable to me in the last decade, here are 4 lessons I've learned from working minimum-wage jobs in the service industry.

## 1\. Plurality of voices leads to better work

If you want a team to get things done and enjoy working together, uniformity is not always your friend. You need a balanced mix of workers of all ages, cultures, skills, ambitions and backgrounds. Keep in mind, this isn't a class on diversity and inclusion, they're lessons based on my own experience. But what I've found is that the teams who work the hardest and seem the happiest are the ones where there's a good mix of people. It's a simplistic statement, but it's true in essence.

I'd love to write more about this in the future. Until then, it’s helpful to know what the research tells. Being part of a diverse team has been found to make your job more fulfilling, as long as your company also makes an effort to include everyone and the leaders support their teams.[1](#footnote-1)[2](#footnote-2)[3](#footnote-3) This correlation between plurality of voices and work that doesn’t suck was most evident in some of the restaurants and bars I worked at in Liverpool and London.

I worked at a burger restaurant in London that no longer exists alongside a talented and well-balanced team of confident, fun, creative and hard-working people. You could hear two or three different languages at any time. My boss was a marine biology graduate. One of the waitresses was training to be a flight attendant (and she ended up with BA not long after I left). Service was smooth even though people had some very different ways of working. We’d have problems but they would get fixed. It was the team that made my 10-hour shifts feel more like 6. I'm not sure exactly how or why, but that multicultural, multiskilled mix of people worked. And it was one of the best jobs I've ever had.

## 2\. Always, always under promise and over deliver deliver exactly what you promised

Managing expectations is a much harder skill than we are willing to admit. It's something I learned at work, but I've found that it applies to every relationship. It's better to tell your chef that you've never baked banana bread before and that you need some help than to promise that they'll get a tasty cake and then deliver a dense, sunk-in-the-middle disappointment. You promise a comfortable level, even when that means doing so with guidance, and you deliver on that comfortable level. This will earn you respect. There's no need to overdeliver either, because you risk maxing out, and people will expect even more next time.

Had I learned this years ago, I’d have saved myself an awful lot of guilt and embarrassment. Even today, as a chronic exaggerator, I have to remind myself of this all the time.

## 3\. CVs and ambitions aren’t good metrics for success

What a person has done so far and their long-term career goals have taken precedence everything in modern recruitment. These criteria, whether explicitly stated by the candidate or assumed by whoever’s hiring, are used to discriminate against candidates, regardless of their readiness to work, transferable skills, and team-fit. What should matter most is how willing you are to put in the work, how effectively you can learn new skills (which doesn't necessarily mean being quick) and how seriously you take the basics — showing up on time, respecting your colleagues, asking for help when you need it, and so on. If, in the first few weeks of employment, a new recruit can prove they are meeting your expectations as an employer, what more do you need?

If it sounds like I'm trying to reduce hiring to simple guidelines for identifying good (enough) candidates, that's exactly what I'm getting at. Who benefits from increasingly hostile and stringent hiring requirements? Certainly not the people looking for jobs, those who'd actually be doing the work. Café and restaurant managers know this better than most. They know that if you want your workforce to do a taxing job for a wage that's often minimum or close to the lowest possible, you can't realistically expect them to have an impeccable CV, to profess some sort of long-held ambition to work for X business, and to stay with you until he end of times.

## 4\. The most rewarding work relationships are with line managers who separate criticism from positive feedback

Fuck the feedback sandwich. Stop constantly criticising your staff. Great managers know how to play to their employees' strengths. The leaders who designed the processes that increased production efficiency after the Industrial Revolution took advantage of one of the greatest strengths of human nature: our tendency to point out flaws. As Jim Clifton and Jim Harter, authors of [Culture Shock](http://orkplace/469790/culture-shock.aspx), wrote, performance management is now “set up to rate and rank employees and focus primarily on their weaknesses. However, this approach fails to improve performance.” People may crave praise at every opportunity, but "constant criticism makes it nearly impossible for a manager and employee to build a healthy relationship."[4](#footnote-4)

Sandwiched feedback erodes trust and it doesn't improve performance despite people thinking it does. [This was proven by a meta-analysis](https://mrbartonmaths.com/resourcesnew/8.%20Research/Marking%20and%20Feedback/The%20effects%20of%20feedback%20interventions.pdf) of 607 feedback studies involving over 23,000 participants 29 years ago (!), and has been reiterated by employees innumerable times since. I must admit that I don't react well to feedback. I can become defensive or anxious when criticised. But these feelings are intensified when criticism is mixed with praise in a way that feels insincere. I want straightforward feedback so I can address issues directly, and I want genuine praise when I've done a great job. Separately. Praise is a transformative tool for good leadership, don’t smear it with criticism.
